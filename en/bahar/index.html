Improve learning by observing evidence and revising small actions.

BAHAR begins with what you actually do when studying. It supports reflection, retrieval practice, feedback and decisions that can be revised without assigning a fixed label to your ability.

Module one · Free

1. Observe your real study habits

Before changing your routine, identify what you currently do. Replace broad judgments about being a “good student” with descriptions of observable activity.

Complete this activity

  1. Choose one manageable topic you studied during the past seven days.
  2. Record what you did: rereading, taking notes, explaining, practicing or testing yourself.
  3. Close the material and recall three key points without looking. This is retrieval practice.
  4. Compare your recall with the source. Identify which points were accurate, incomplete or unclear.
  5. Note a relevant contextual factor: time, sleep, interruptions, access to materials or assistance.
Worked example

Topic: the water cycle. Initial activity: reread twice. Retrieval: accurately recalled two of three stages. Next action: practice the missing stage with a short question.

Why retrieval differs from familiarity

Rereading may create a feeling of familiarity, while trying to recall ideas without the source provides more direct learning evidence. Explain a concept aloud, solve a short question or sketch a concept map. Then compare your answer with a reliable resource or feedback from a teacher. Errors are not judgments about intelligence. Sleep, language, time, prior knowledge and access to support can also affect a particular sample of performance.

Complete worksheet: Topic, retrieval, feedback and next step
  1. Choose one limited concept and describe your current study method.
  2. Answer three specific questions without looking at the material; mark uncertain answers.
  3. Check the responses against a reliable source and find an example for each gap.
  4. Choose one small action for your next study session and consider the actual circumstances in which it will occur.

This completes an introductory activity with revisable evidence, not a psychometric assessment or prediction of future success.

One activity cannot establish a person's overall intelligence, talent or learning potential.

Module two · Free

2. Design a small learning experiment

The point is not to prescribe a single fixed study method. Try one achievable change and decide what to retain or revise on the basis of evidence.

Complete this activity

  1. Choose a modest, observable goal for the next week.
  2. Specify one concrete action, such as ten minutes of closed-book recall after study.
  3. Choose a feasible time and setting that reflect your actual circumstances.
  4. Specify the evidence you will collect: responses, errors, feedback or work samples.
  5. Review the evidence at the end of the week and choose to continue, modify, pause or seek support.
Worked example

Goal: remember unit vocabulary more reliably. Action: do three five-minute retrieval sessions this week. Evidence: list recalled words at each session. Review: examine whether varied examples or spacing make a useful difference.

The study-action cycle: Act, observe and revise

Define a human-authored, observable goal. Select a feasible small action, gather examples of what happened and decide whether to continue, change or pause. No single technique is automatically optimal for every learner or topic. Time, access, personal circumstances and professional support should inform the decision.

Complete one-week experiment worksheet
  1. Goal: Specify a limited outcome, such as explaining three ideas or solving two problem types.
  2. Action: Schedule several brief opportunities for retrieval practice.
  3. Evidence: Keep samples of responses, errors and feedback rather than assigning fixed labels to yourself.
  4. Review: Ask what changed and what remains uncertain.
  5. Decision: Explicitly choose to continue, change, pause, finish or investigate further, explaining why.

Adapt the experiment if it conflicts with health or family circumstances. When evidence is insufficient or problems persist, ask a teacher or qualified professional for support.
