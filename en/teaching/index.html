Teaching philosophy in practice

Evidence of learning is more informative than the appearance of coverage.

In university teaching, covering the planned material does not necessarily establish that students can use it. A course in research methods, for example, may require students not only to recognise methodological terms but also to justify a design choice, identify the limits of a dataset and explain why a particular inference is warranted. The difference becomes visible through tasks that ask learners to make and defend decisions rather than simply reproduce definitions.

Such an approach calls for constructive alignment among learning outcomes, classroom activity, feedback and assessment. It also requires flexibility: where students’ prior knowledge or available resources differ, the instructional route may need adjustment without abandoning the intellectual standard. These are principles guiding teaching decisions, not claims of measured effectiveness in the absence of a documented evaluation.

Teaching philosophy

The classroom is not a delivery mechanism.

I do not understand teaching as the efficient movement of content from one side of a room to the other. A course matters when students can make distinctions they could not make before, ask better questions, use knowledge in unfamiliar situations and gradually assume more responsibility for their own judgement. This is also why assessment, materials and feedback cannot be separated from teaching: each one shapes what learners come to regard as important.

Academic context and interpretation

Professional judgement is exercised in relation to actual learners

The apparent completion of a syllabus does not necessarily mean that its central ideas have become usable knowledge for students. A learner may recognise an explanation yet struggle to retrieve or apply it in a different task, while an unsuccessful answer may reflect the conditions of performance rather than a stable personal limitation. Teaching therefore involves interpreting the evidence of learning in relation to task design, opportunity, prior experience and the feedback that follows.

This is one reason why classroom work continues to inform the broader educational argument underlying Golden Talent. BAHAR can be read as a proposed movement from understanding a situation to selecting an action, monitoring its effects and revising the approach. Its significance in teaching is conceptual and practical; the effectiveness of any particular intervention must still be established within the circumstances in which it is used.

For a fuller account of the proposed framework and its limitations, see Golden Talent and the methodology.

Teacher professional development: what should change after the workshop?

Professional learning is sometimes assessed through attendance, the number of workshops completed or the acquisition of certificates. These measures can document participation, but they do not, in themselves, establish that teachers have reconsidered a decision or that learners have encountered better opportunities to learn. The more demanding question is whether a professional learning experience enables a teacher to interpret classroom evidence differently and to test a worthwhile change within the constraints of that setting.

The Education Endowment Foundation's guidance on effective professional development (2021) identifies mechanisms such as goal-setting, feedback, revisiting prior learning and action planning that may contribute to changes in practice. The earlier best-evidence synthesis by Timperley and colleagues (2007) likewise foregrounds the relationship between teacher learning, the interpretation of professional knowledge and the educational outcomes valued in particular contexts. Taken together, these perspectives suggest that a session's topic alone is an insufficient basis for judging its professional value; what teachers are able to do with the experience is equally important.

The distinction remains practically significant. In the OECD's TALIS 2024 findings, published in 2025, approximately 55% of teachers across participating OECD education systems reported that their recent professional learning had a positive impact on their teaching. This is a self-reported indicator, rather than a causal estimate of improvements in student achievement. Nevertheless, it illustrates why professional development cannot be assumed effective merely because a teacher has participated in it.

A five-question reflection that teachers can use without signing in

  1. Which learning outcome in this particular class remains difficult to achieve?
  2. What evidence of student activity, understanding or assessment supports that concern?
  3. Which contextual or instructional explanations remain plausible, and what evidence might distinguish them?
  4. What small, feasible change could be attempted without disrupting essential classroom commitments?
  5. What would count as meaningful evidence for retaining, revising or abandoning that change?

How the questions might inform an English language classroom

Consider a teacher whose learners complete written exercises but contribute little to spoken discussion. A premature response would be to attribute the problem entirely to motivation or to prescribe more speaking tasks. A more context-sensitive inquiry might examine who receives opportunities to speak, how long learners have to prepare, whether the task provides adequate linguistic support and how feedback shapes willingness to participate. The teacher could then introduce one manageable change, observe its consequences and reconsider the initial explanation. This hypothetical case is not evidence of a particular intervention's effectiveness; it illustrates a form of professional reasoning.

Such a process does not imply that every teacher or institution requires consultancy. In some cases, peer discussion and careful examination of existing classroom evidence may be sufficient. Where the issue is more complex, a clearly bounded professional conversation may help the teacher distinguish what is known, what remains uncertain and what action is justified. That is a more defensible starting point for professional development than assuming that one programme or technique should work in every classroom.
